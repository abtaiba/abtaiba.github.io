Pattern Recognition and Tactic Classification in Rocq

Can we learn the structure of proofs? A new dataset of Rocq proof segments from Fiat-Crypto, labelled by tactic category, used to test twelve classifiers.

The question

In Rocq (formerly Coq), a proof is a script of tactics: commands such as intros, simpl or apply that transform the goal step by step until it is proved. Rocq labels code as theorems and lemmas, but those labels say nothing about how a proof actually works.

Can a classifier learn how proofs are structured? This project builds a dataset that labels pieces of proofs by the kind of tactics they use, then tests whether classifiers can learn those labels. The longer-term aim is to look at an incomplete proof and predict which kind of tactic belongs in the gap.

Data

The dataset comes from Fiat-Crypto, MIT CSAIL's Coq formalisation of cryptographic primitives, with nearly 8,000 commits. I parsed every proof into segments: contiguous blocks of tactics working on a single subgoal, split at bullets, braces and indentation. Each segment records its tactics, nesting depth, position in the proof and parent theorem.

That gives 4,328 segments from 263 files and 1,582 theorems. Each tactic is mapped to one of seven groups (below), and a segment is labelled with its most frequent group. Segments that only use Fiat-Crypto's own custom tactics can't be mapped, so they are dropped as unknown, leaving 3,907 labelled segments.

Tactic groups

  • rewrite-reduce: simplifies terms without closing goals (simpl, cbn, unfold, subst, autorewrite). The largest group, because Fiat-Crypto does heavy symbolic field arithmetic.
  • hypothesis-management: organises the available hypotheses without changing the goal (intros, clear, revert, set, assert).
  • application: closes goals by matching a hypothesis, lemma or constructor (apply, exact, auto, split, constructor).
  • case-induction: splits goals into subgoals and gives proofs their tree shape (induction, destruct, inversion).
  • contradiction: closes goals with inconsistent hypotheses or a false goal (contradiction, exfalso, congruence).
  • arithmetic-algebra: automated arithmetic and algebra solvers, used only in specific situations (ring, field, lia, nra).
  • meta-tactical: combinators that add control flow around other tactics (try, repeat, solve, now).

Nearly every declaration uses most groups somewhere, but within a single segment one group usually dominates, and that is what the labels capture. The labels are heavily imbalanced:

Features

Each segment has over 70 features in five groups:

  • Structure: segment length, proof length, and how far through the proof the segment sits.
  • Composition: the share of the segment's tactics in each of the seven groups.
  • Derived: entropy of that mix, number of branches, tactic diversity, and the share of recognised tactics.
  • Proof context: the same mix and entropy across the whole proof, plus its number of segments.
  • Tactic flags: one yes/no flag for each of the 50 most common tactics.

Experiments and results

Twelve classifiers are compared: decision tree, random forest, bagging, logistic regression, linear perceptron, KNN, Parzen window, naive Bayes, Gaussian MLE, Bayesian estimation, Bayes minimum risk, and a voting ensemble. The data is split 70/15/15 into training, validation and test sets, and predictions are scored against the segment labels described above.

Three runs, reported as test accuracy:

  • All 70+ features: tree-based and linear models do very well (bagging 99.5%, decision tree 99.0%, logistic regression 98.5%), but the probabilistic classifiers collapse (naive Bayes 26.5%, Bayes minimum risk 26.3%, Bayesian estimation 51.7%).
  • Reduced with PCA (principal component analysis): the three probabilistic classifiers recover to 67–72%, while most of the strong ones drop. Logistic regression stays best at 96.4%.
  • Reduced with MDA (multiple discriminant analysis, down to just 6 features): every classifier lands between 91% and 96%, with random forest highest at 96.4%.

Some classifiers were also given class-weighted versions to account for the imbalanced labels.

Limitations

Fiat-Crypto is small for this: about 1,500 theorems give about 4,000 segments, with a lot of duplication. The high scores could come from the feature engineering, or from the task being too easy, and features that work for Fiat-Crypto may not suit other Rocq projects. The natural next step is to run the same pipeline on other repositories. More proof-aware feature selection would need manual labelling and metaprogramming, which is out of scope for now.
